# Copjacketing and the Manufacture of Suspicion: Agent Provocateurs, COINTELPRO, and the Political Technology of Internal Fracture

## 1. Introduction: from cinematic provocation to documentary “covert action”

A recurring story about protest repression claims that undercover police *create* visible violence in order to justify escalation. In its strongest form, the story imagines an undercover officer committing an incendiary act in the crowd, thereby “proving” the protest is violent and furnishing a pretext for riot-police repression.

This paper does not treat that scenario as impossible in principle. Instead, it asks a narrower question: what do the most authoritative U.S. government disclosures about domestic counterintelligence *actually document* as routine or characteristic?

The clearest documentary record remains the U.S. Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities (1975–76), commonly called the Church Committee. Its work (and related staff reports) describes COINTELPRO as a set of covert action programs directed against domestic groups, extending beyond intelligence collection into secret action meant to “disrupt” and “neutralize” targets [@senateChurchCommittee]. It explicitly frames this as “covert action,” not merely investigation [@church1976b3, p. 3].

The central claim advanced here is:

1. The best-documented COINTELPRO practice is not “manufacturing public justification,” but manufacturing *internal suspicion*.
2. The contemporary movement-level practice of accusing others of being police (often without evidence) is best named as *copjacketing*—and it is structurally aligned with what COINTELPRO disclosures describe as “snitch-jacketing.”

A compact analytic model is:

\[
\text{Neutralization} \approx \text{Disruption} + \text{Discrediting} + \text{Manufactured Suspicion}.
\]

## 2. Definitions and distinctions

## 2.1. Infiltration

“Infiltration” refers to the placement of informants or undercover actors inside a group to gather information. The Church Committee explicitly treats infiltration as part of intelligence *collection* [@church1976b2, p. 1].

## 2.2. Agent provocateur

An agent provocateur is an infiltrator who pushes a group toward illegal or unpopular acts, either to justify repression or to generate prosecutable offenses. Conceptually, this overlaps with the legal doctrine of entrapment (Section 2.3), but it is not identical: provocation may be political rather than evidentiary.

## 2.3. Entrapment (legal doctrine)

The DOJ describes entrapment as a defense that applies when government agents “originate a criminal design,” implant disposition, and induce commission so the government may prosecute [@dojEntrapment645]. This matters because many “agent provocateur” stories silently assume the state needs an *evidentiary* pathway (charges, convictions), rather than merely a *political* or *administrative* pathway (crowd control, surveillance, intimidation).

## 2.4. Copjacketing

In movement vernacular, “copjacketing” is the practice of labeling or implying that a person is a cop, informant, or agent—typically on thin evidence, and often circulated publicly. It is both (a) a descriptive claim about someone’s identity and (b) a disciplinary act with material consequences (exclusion, harassment, threats).

Copjacketing can be “true” in the narrow sense that infiltrators exist; it can still be politically catastrophic as a *practice* if it becomes rumor-driven and self-reproducing.

## 2.5. Snitch-jacketing (COINTELPRO disclosure term)

The Church Committee staff report describes “snitch jacket” as “falsely labeling a group member as an informant” [@church1976b3, p. 7]. It later describes it operationally as neutralizing a target by labeling them an informant so they are no longer trusted, and notes methods ranging from rumor to anonymous letters to faked informant reports [@church1976b3, p. 45].

The report further states that this technique “was used in all COINTELPROs” [@church1976b3, p. 45].

This provides a direct historical bridge: what movements call “copjacketing” has a documented *state* analogue: deliberate covert manufacture of informant accusations to fracture trust.

## 3. Method and evidentiary scope

This paper is a documentary synthesis. It privileges:
- Church Committee materials (Books II and III) as primary disclosures [@church1976b2; @church1976b3].
- FBI’s public COINTELPRO portal as an institutional acknowledgment and entry point to FOIA releases [@fbiVaultCointelpro].
- DOJ’s archived articulation of entrapment elements as a legal frame frequently conflated with “provocation” [@dojEntrapment645].
- A mainstream institutional explanation of “outside agitator” rhetoric as a comparative frame for “blame outsiders” narratives [@apOutsideAgitators].

Limitations: this draft does not claim to trace the single origin point of the “undercover commits spectacular violence” story. Instead, it (a) isolates what is *documented* and (b) proposes a research agenda for origin-tracing (Section 9).

## 4. What the Church Committee discloses COINTELPRO *was*

The Church Committee’s final report distinguishes three types of intelligence activity: collection, dissemination, and “covert action designed to disrupt and discredit” [@church1976b2, p. 1]. COINTELPRO is treated as a major case of this third type [@church1976b2, p. 9; @senateChurchCommittee].

The Book III staff report underscores that “covert action” is the more accurate term for these domestic programs [@church1976b3, p. 3]. That is, the point was not simply learning about movements; it was intervening in them without attribution.

Within that frame, the staff report catalogs a spectrum of tactics, including (among others) “snitch jacket” and even “encouraging street warfare between violent groups” [@church1976b3, p. 7]. The analytic point is not that every tactic was always used, but that internal documents normalized a menu of interventions whose success metric was disruption and neutralization.

## 5. The documented core: manufacturing suspicion (snitch-jacketing)

## 5.1. The tactic in one sentence

The staff report describes “snitch jacket” as “neutralizing a target by labeling him a ‘snitch’ or informant” [@church1976b3, p. 45]. That is, the state does not need to *prove* someone is an informant; it needs others to *believe* it.

## 5.2. Why this is strategically powerful

Snitch-jacketing exploits three predictable properties of clandestine or embattled organizing:
1. Groups must manage risk under uncertainty.
2. Trust is costly to build and easy to destroy.
3. Accusations are faster than investigations.

In other words, the state can “purchase” disruption cheaply by injecting low-cost claims that impose high-cost verification burdens on the target community.

## 5.3. A COINTELPRO typology consistent with “manufactured suspicion”

The Church Committee report gives concrete examples of covert tactics that are best read as information warfare:
- Anonymous letters to spouses to produce personal rupture [@church1976b2, p. 9].
- False labeling as informants to invite expulsion or attack [@church1976b2, p. 9].
- “Misinformation” to disrupt demonstrations (e.g., fake orders on citizens band radio; falsified housing forms to waste demonstrators’ time) [@church1976b2, p. 9].

Notably, these are not “pretext creation” tactics aimed at convincing the public that police violence is justified. They are tactics aimed at:
- degrading internal cohesion,
- exhausting organizational capacity,
- and redirecting movement attention away from goals and into damage control.

## 5.4. Case vignette: “cast further suspicion”

In a Black Panther Party-related example, an FBI memorandum in the staff report indicates anonymous letters were proposed to “cast further suspicion” on a chapter leader by labeling him a “fink,” explicitly framed as a way to create internal distrust [@church1976b3, p. 46].

The relevant analytic point is the mechanism: it is *not* a provocation toward public violence for media optics. It is a covert claim meant to break trust, which can then produce cascading internal harms (splits, expulsions, retaliatory violence, reputational damage).

## 5.5. Case vignette: marriage sabotage as “neutralization”

The staff report also documents the use of anonymous communications aimed at producing “ill feeling and possibly a lasting distrust” between spouses [@church1976b3, p. 53]. This makes “neutralization” legible as a broad social technology: attacks on relationships, not just arrests.

## 5.6. Why “copjacketing” is the correct name for the movement-level echo

When movement participants, under the influence of a popular narrative, publicly accuse direct-action participants of being police with little evidence, the effect often resembles the COINTELPRO tactic more than it resembles resistance. It isolates people, fractures coalitions, and can invite harassment or violence.

This is the key reversal: a story about “police infiltration” can itself become a *vehicle* for the very neutralization practice it fears.

## 6. Agent provocateurs: plausible, but not the main disclosure signal

The Church Committee materials certainly describe informants and undercover activity, and they catalogue tactics that can raise controversial issues “to cause dissent” [@church1976b3, p. 7]. But the most explicit, repeatedly emphasized tactic is snitch-jacketing: labeling others as informants to neutralize them [@church1976b3, p. 45].

The legal concept of entrapment helps clarify what is and is not being alleged when people invoke “agent provocateurs.” If someone claims an undercover officer pushed others into illegal acts so the government could prosecute, that begins to resemble the DOJ’s entrapment frame [@dojEntrapment645]. But many protest crackdowns do not depend on a careful prosecution pipeline; they depend on administrative control, political messaging, and force.

This distinction matters for movement education: if you treat every escalation as “manufactured to create a pretext,” you may miss what disclosures emphasize as the more routine mechanism—internal disruption and delegitimation.

## 7. The “undercover throws the Molotov” story as a movement myth-technology

This paper treats “undercover commits spectacular violence to justify escalation” as a *myth-technology* in two senses:

1. It is a narrative tool that offers a satisfying explanatory arc (escalation → hidden instigator → crackdown).
2. It is socially useful: it makes it easier to redirect blame away from police discretion and toward a stigmatized “outside” figure.

That second property is what links it to two older, durable frames:

- The “outside agitator” trope, in which authorities attribute protests to outsiders to delegitimize local grievances and justify repression [@apOutsideAgitators].
- The snitch-jacketing tactic, in which covert actors seed informant accusations to fracture solidarity [@church1976b3, p. 45].

In both cases, the narrative’s political effect is to transform a conflict with institutions into a conflict inside the movement.

## 8. Curriculum implications: what to teach “inbound” organizers

This section is written as curriculum material: not a list of operational security tricks, but an epistemic and organizational discipline aimed at reducing self-neutralization.

## 8.1. Teach the taxonomy, not the movie scene

A practical baseline module is to teach organizers the difference between:
- infiltration (information gathering),
- provocation (pushing illegal acts),
- entrapment (legal inducement toward prosecutable crime),
- disinformation (false or manipulative messaging),
- and snitch-jacketing/copjacketing (weaponized accusation).

The Church Committee framing of “covert action designed to disrupt and discredit” is useful here: it tells learners to look for internal disruption signatures, not only for dramatic acts [@church1976b2, p. 1].

## 8.2. Treat “cop” accusations as a high-risk act, not casual commentary

Because COINTELPRO disclosures show that false informant labeling can be an intentional tactic [@church1976b2, p. 9; @church1976b3, p. 45], public accusations should be treated as:
- materially dangerous to the accused,
- strategically beneficial to adversaries,
- and epistemically hard to validate.

## 8.3. Replace rumor-cycles with accountable processes

“Accountable” does not mean paranoid; it means:
- clear norms about evidence,
- clear boundaries against doxxing and vigilantism,
- and clear pathways for addressing concerns without public spectacle.

The historical lesson of snitch-jacketing is that suspicion can be *manufactured*; therefore, an organization’s “immune system” must be designed not to overreact to low-quality signals.

## 8.4. Re-center the locus of agency

The “agent provocateur creates a pretext” story can inadvertently grant police too much narrative power while obscuring a more banal reality: institutions can and do escalate without needing theatrical justification. In that sense, the story may become a way of avoiding the harder political analysis: repression is often a choice, not a response.

## 9. Research agenda for a deep-research agent: tracing the “Molotov cop” genealogy

Because this draft is document-driven, it ends with a concrete research plan rather than a speculative origin story.

1. **Earliest attestations in print (U.S.)**
   - Search newspaper archives, trial transcripts, and early internet forums for the earliest phrase-clusters:
     - “undercover” + “Molotov” + “protest”
     - “agent provocateur” + “Molotov”
     - “police” + “threw” + “firebomb” + “to justify”
   - Extract date, location, and whether the claim cites documents or is rumor.

2. **Differentiate three claim-types**
   - (A) undercover commits violence *against police* to enable extraction;
   - (B) undercover commits violence *against property/people* to prompt escalation;
   - (C) third parties commit violence and are labeled police without evidence (copjacketing).

3. **Cross-validate with institutional disclosures**
   - Compare claim clusters to the Church Committee’s documented menu: disinformation, anonymous letters, false informant labeling, and other “covert action” tactics [@church1976b2; @church1976b3].

4. **Study memetic spread events**
   - Identify moments (major uprisings, heavily mediated protests) where the narrative becomes common.
   - Analyze the platform mechanics (virality, clip culture, doxxing incentives) that turn suspicion into public identification campaigns.

This is the point at which a deep research agent (with archive access) can convert the agenda into a rigorous genealogy.

## 10. Conclusion

The Church Committee disclosures about COINTELPRO depict a domestic counterintelligence program whose characteristic move is “covert action designed to disrupt and discredit,” not merely investigation [@church1976b2, p. 1; @senateChurchCommittee]. Within that repertoire, the “snitch jacket” stands out as a named and explicitly described practice: falsely labeling targets as informants to neutralize them by destroying trust [@church1976b3, pp. 7, 45]. The final report likewise records false informant labeling as a tactic that could expose targets to expulsion or physical attack [@church1976b2, p. 9].

In that documentary context, “copjacketing” is not only an accusation people make; it is a historically attested *technology of repression*—one that can be reproduced unintentionally by movements when rumor replaces evidence and public spectacle replaces accountable process.

The pedagogical imperative is therefore not “deny infiltration exists,” but: teach the documented history in a way that makes movements harder to fracture by the manufacture of suspicion.
