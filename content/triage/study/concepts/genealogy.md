# An Incomplete Genealogy of Concepts

This genealogy traces how concepts are treated in philosophy: from early
accounts of universals and abstraction to modern semantic and cognitive models.

Key strands include:

- classical treatments of universals and categories,
- early modern distinctions between ideas and concepts,
- analytic philosophy's focus on meaning, reference, and intension,
- cognitive approaches that model concept formation and use.

## Conceptual genealogy (Foucauldian practice)

Conceptual genealogy is a method for tracing how a concept becomes possible and
dominant within a field of discourse. It does not search for a pure origin.
Instead, it maps contingencies, institutional forces, and shifts in practice
that shape what can be said, thought, and done.

Core commitments include:

- treating concepts as products of discourse, not timeless essences,
- looking for discontinuities and breaks rather than linear progress,
- attending to power relations that stabilize or contest meanings,
- analyzing archives, institutions, and practices that organize knowledge.

A minimal workflow:

1. Define the concept and its contemporary use.
2. Identify the archive (texts, policies, tools, and practices) where the concept
   is formed and repeated.
3. Track how the concept is linked to institutions, expertise, and authority.
4. Note discontinuities: moments when the concept shifts, splits, or is replaced.
5. Summarize how the concept's conditions of possibility changed over time.
